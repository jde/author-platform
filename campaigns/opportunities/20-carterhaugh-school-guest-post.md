Hi Sara and Brittany,

I enjoyed Daniel Rabuzzi's essay on Scandinavian folklore in English-language fantasy on your site. I'd like to propose a related guest essay on how those folklore beings appeared in Swedish court records.

Between 1600 and 1800, Swedish courts tried farmers and milkmaids for relations with Näcken, a water spirit, and the Skogsrå, a forest spirit. I spent years in those records, and in my novel *The Lure of Water and Wood* the two spirits narrate the trials.

The essay would be about 1,200 words, written for your site: how a folklore being came to be treated as legal evidence, what the records say about the Skogsrå, and how I wrote her as a character. I can include two images, and I don't charge a fee.

### Essay outline for a 1,200-word essay
Working title: The Spirits Were on Trial Too

1. Opening (150 words): a specific trial record, a man in [parish, year; Helen: pick the case] confessing that the Skogsrå met him in the forest. The court did not question whether she existed. It asked what he had given her.

2. What the records say about the beings (250 words): Näcken as the fiddler in the stream, and as an explanation a man could give for where he had been overnight. The Skogsrå as a beautiful woman with a hollow back, associated with both harm and luck for hunters, and with sexual accusations. Blåkulla as the place children described in the 1668 to 1676 trials, with testimony shaped by the same questions in each parish. The courts used village beliefs to define guilt.

3. The problem for a novelist (250 words): The sources are hostile: confessions under duress, children's testimony coached by clergy, and no account from the beings' side. The options are to write only the humans and treat the spirits as delusion, to write the spirits as monsters, or to have the spirits narrate as part of village life. Why she chose the third: it gives the accused agency without minimizing what happened to them, and it reflects how villagers lived alongside these beliefs.

4. Writing the Skogsrå as a character (250 words): In folklore she reflects men's fears. In the trial records she is also a woman in the forest. How the novel gives her her own wants, a view of the men, and a stake in whether the trials end.

5. How the trials ended (200 words): the 1676 commission and the end of the great hunt. Where the novel fits among the modern books Rabuzzi's essay discusses.

6. Closing (100 words): The Foremothers Café, where she hosts talks on these records in VR, with an invitation to attend one and a single link.

Byline: 60-word bio. Two images with credits.

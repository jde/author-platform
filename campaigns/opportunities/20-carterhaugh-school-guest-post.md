Subject: A guest essay: the Skogsrå and Näcken as co-accused in Sweden's sorcery trials

Hi Sara and Brittany,

Daniel Rabuzzi's essay on Scandinavian folklore in English fantasy is the best map of that shelf I've read, and it leaves one road untaken: what happened when the beings turned up in court.

Between 1600 and 1800, Swedish courts tried farmers and milkmaids for relations with Näcken, the fiddler in the river, and the Skogsrå, the woman of the woods. The folklore was in the charge sheet. I spent years in those records; in *The Lure of Water and Wood* the two beings narrate the trials.

For your readers I'd write an exclusive 1,200-word essay, "The Spirits Were on Trial Too": how a folklore being becomes legal evidence, what the records say the Skogsrå did, and how I wrote her without a warning label. Two images, no fee.

If it's not your season, I'll keep reading the newsletter anyway.

Best,
Helen Lundström Erwin

### Essay outline, 400 words of outline for a 1,200-word essay
Working title: The Spirits Were on Trial Too

1. Open in the courtroom, not the forest (150 words). A specific record: a man in [parish, year; Helen: pick the case] confessing that the Skogsrå came to him among the trees. The court does not ask whether she exists. It asks what he gave her.

2. What the records say the beings did (250 words). Näcken as the fiddler in the stream and as an alibi: a man "taken" by him was not answerable for where he had been all night. The Skogsrå as the beautiful woman with a hollow back who ruined hunters and blessed them; the sexual charge and the legal one arriving together. Blåkulla as the destination in the 1668 to 1676 trials, where children's testimony was assembled from the same questions in every parish. The folklore and the law were one conversation; the court used the village's beliefs as the frame for guilt.

3. The problem for a novelist (250 words). The sources are hostile: confessions under duress, testimony from children coached by clergy, no voice for the beings at all. Three choices: write the humans and treat the spirits as delusion; write the spirits as monsters; or let the spirits narrate and treat them as participants in village life. Why the third: it returns agency to the accused without pretending the terror was staged, and it matches how villagers actually lived alongside the beings, which was as neighbors.

4. Writing the Skogsrå without a warning label (250 words). The folklore's seductress is a male fear with a tail. In the trial records she is also simply a woman walking in the forest, which the court could not forgive. How the novel gives her ordinary wants, a view of the men, and a stake in whether the trials end.

5. What Sweden did next (200 words). The commission of 1676, the end of the great hunt, and the beings going back to being folklore. Rabuzzi's map of the modern shelf; where a 752-page novel narrated by a water spirit sits on it.

6. Close with the fika (100 words). The Foremothers Café, where she now hosts talks on these records in VR, and an invitation to come hear one. One link.
